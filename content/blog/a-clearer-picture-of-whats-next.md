Ask five members when formal is and you might get three answers. The social chair put a date in the semester spreadsheet. The exec notes say it moved. Someone in the group chat is sure it's the week after, and forty messages later nobody has settled it.

Most orgs don't have a scheduling problem. They have a *where* problem. Dates live in a Google Sheet that was last updated in September, a doc called "Fall plan FINAL v3", a pinned message nobody can find, and the president's head. Each one is a little out of date in its own way, so members stop trusting any of them and ask in the chat instead. The same question gets answered again every week, and the person who missed that answer misses the event.

The Timeline page gives every date your organization keeps one home. Meetings, socials, service days, and deadlines sit in order on a single line that every member can open, kept current by the officers who plan them. When something moves, it moves in one place, and everyone sees the new date.

The interactive examples in this post use a fictional chapter. Start below: these are the places one semester's dates were living. Gather them and see what's left.

## Add an event: put it on the line

Adding an event takes the details people actually need: what it is, what type it is, when, and where. Mark it **required** if it counts toward attendance. Chapter meetings are required by default, and anything else can be switched on when it matters.

A title, a time, and a room are enough for a weekly meeting. They're not enough for a formal, a service trip, or a tailgate, where people need to know what to wear, where to park, and who's bringing the cooler. That's the event form's second page, **More details**: a drawer of small, purpose-built pieces like a checklist, a budget that adds itself up, a "who's bringing what" sign-up, a point of contact, a dress code, directions, or a field you name yourself. ChaptOS suggests the ones that fit the event's type first. Add only what the event needs, and skip the page entirely when it needs nothing.

Press **Add event** and the form folds into the event's own card, flies to its date, and gets stamped onto the timeline. You see exactly where it landed and what sits around it, which is often the moment someone notices that the philanthropy 5K and the alumni dinner are on the same Saturday. There is no separate "publish" step. Once it's on the timeline, it's on every member's timeline, in date order.

Open the card and you see it the way members do: the dress code next to the time and place, who to text with questions, and a sign-up list they can claim from. Planning pieces like the checklist and the budget stay with officers. If every event of a type needs the same details, save them to the type and new events start with them already in place.

*Change anything you like on the event, open More details to add a checklist or a budget, then press Add event and watch it land on the timeline.*

## Event types: make the categories yours

Every org runs different things. A fraternity has chapter, socials, and rush. A club soccer team has practices, games, and travel weekends. An honor society has inductions and tutoring hours. A generic "Event" label tells members nothing about what they're signing up for.

Under **Settings → Event types**, officers name the categories their org actually uses and give each one a color. Those colors carry through the whole timeline: the dot on the spine, the label on the card, and the legend at the top, so a glance down the page tells you which weeks are heavy on service and which ones are all deadlines.

A few types are built in because other parts of ChaptOS depend on them. Chapter meetings feed attendance, and deadlines come straight from Tasks. You can rename and recolor those, and add as many of your own as you need. A custom type can be **required by default**, so a team that tracks practice attendance doesn't have to tick the box every time.

*Edit a type to rename or recolor it, add one of your own, then filter the timeline by type.*

## Add to calendar: in the place people already look

However good the timeline is, members live in their own calendars. Their classes are there, their shifts are there, and that's where they'll notice a conflict. So ChaptOS puts your org's events there too.

**Add to my calendar** subscribes a member's Google or Apple Calendar to the org's timeline in about a minute. Every meeting and deadline appears alongside their own schedule, in your org's type colors, and when an officer moves an event, their calendar picks up the change on its next refresh. Nobody has to re-send an invite or remember to update anything.

For people who would rather not subscribe, any single event can be added as a **one-time copy** to Google Calendar or downloaded for Apple and others. A copy is a snapshot. It won't follow along if the event changes, and ChaptOS says so right next to the button.

*Pick your calendar and press the button. The chapter's events land on the phone beside your own.*

## Live check-in: attendance in the moment

For required events, attendance shouldn't mean the secretary reading fifty names off a list while everyone waits. When the meeting starts, an officer taps **Open check-in** on the event, and members check themselves in from their own phones with a single **I'm here**.

The window stays open for an hour, and members see it wherever they are in the app, not only on the dashboard. Officers watch the tally climb with the newest arrivals at the top. Someone who can't make it can file an excuse from the check-in itself instead of texting an officer.

When the window closes, the result becomes the event's attendance record: who was present, who was excused, and who was absent, counted toward each member's attendance standing. The roll is still there afterward for officers to correct a forgotten phone or a late arrival.

*Open check-in, tap I'm here, and watch the room fill. Close the window to record attendance.*

## One place to look

None of these features is the point on its own. The point is that a member wondering what's next has one place to check, and that the answer there is right. Officers plan in the same place members read, so the timeline stays current without anyone maintaining a second copy.

The group chat can go back to being for conversation. The spreadsheet can retire. And the next time someone asks when formal is, the answer is a link.
